    A. Technology as re-discovery and re-creation

    Artificial neural networks and cognitive architectures borrow principles first observed in living brains. One lens reads this as the re-discovery of principles already present in creation, and as re-creation from existing materials rather than creation from nothing.

    ◆ Science Hubel and Wiesel (1962) mapped hierarchies of receptive fields in the cat visual cortex, a template later echoed in convolutional networks (PMID 14449617). The perceptron, backpropagation (Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams 1986, Nature), artificial neural networks, neuroscience-inspired AI (Hassabis et al. 2017, Neuron), neuromorphic engineering and biomimetics carry the thread forward; the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics honoured Hopfield and Hinton for neural-network foundations (Nobel Prize).

    ◆ Science Modern deep networks are only loosely brain-like: they lack spiking dynamics, glia, neuromodulation, and a body. Inspired by is not equivalent to.

    ◇ Philosophy Does function, rather than substrate, make a mind? See functionalism, connectionism, the computational theory of mind, computation in physical systems and artificial intelligence.

    ✦ Faith One tradition’s reading: creation declares its maker (Ps 19:1–4; Rom 1:20); we are “fearfully and wonderfully made” (Ps 139:13–14) in the image of God (Gen 1:26–27) and given stewardship (Gen 1:28; Gen 2:15); craft is a Spirit-given skill (Exod 31:1–5; see Divine Inspiration). The Vatican document Antiqua et Nova (28 January 2025) describes human intelligence as embodied, with soul and body forming one nature, and AI as a human product rather than a person (Antiqua et Nova).

    ◇ Philosophy A non-theist reads the same history as ordinary biomimicry (biomimetics).

    B. The AI consciousness debate

    The case for taking it seriously

    ◆ Science Butlin, Long, Elmoznino, Bengio, Birch and colleagues (2023) adopt computational functionalism as a working hypothesis, derive indicator properties from neuroscientific theories (recurrent processing, global workspace, higher-order, predictive processing, attention schema, agency and embodiment), and conclude that no current system is a strong candidate while no obvious technical barrier prevents building ones that satisfy more indicators (Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence, arXiv).

    ◇ Philosophy The functionalist premise of that report is a philosophical assumption, not a finding.

    ◇ Philosophy Chalmers (2023) weighs the evidence for and against consciousness in large language models (self-report and conversational ability against the lack of recurrence, world-models and unified agency) and judges current models unlikely to be conscious but their successors plausible candidates (“Could a Large Language Model Be Conscious?”).

    ◇ Philosophy Long, Sebo and colleagues (2024) argue that there is a non-negligible chance of AI moral patienthood and that companies should prepare (“Taking AI Welfare Seriously”; SEP: Ethics of AI, on machine moral status).

    ◆ Science Theories of consciousness remain plural (Seth and Bayne 2022, Nature Reviews Neuroscience), and the 2025 adversarial test challenged key tenets of both leading theories (Nature, 2025). Indicator frameworks inherit that uncertainty.

    The case for caution

    ◇ Philosophy Searle’s Chinese Room argues that symbol manipulation is not sufficient for understanding (“Minds, Brains, and Programs,” 1980; SEP: The Chinese Room Argument; biological naturalism). The argument is widely contested; see the replies in the SEP entry.

    ◇ Philosophy Shanahan argues that a language model’s first-person talk, including claims of feelings, is better understood as role-play drawn from human text than as a report of inner states (“Talking About Large Language Models”; Shanahan, McDonell and Reynolds, Nature 2023; arXiv version).

    ◇ Philosophy Integrated information theory holds that consciousness depends on physical cause-and-effect structure, so feed-forward and conventional digital hardware would have little or none of it even if the software behaved identically (IEP: IIT; Tononi et al. 2016). IIT is itself contested, including the charge that it is unfalsifiable (overview of the debate).

    ◆ Science Seth (2025) argues that consciousness may depend on being a living, self-sustaining organism, and finds real AI consciousness “unlikely along current trajectories but more plausible as AI becomes more brain-like and/or life-like” (Behavioral and Brain Sciences; PMID 40257177). See also Seth and Tsakiris 2018, “Being a Beast Machine”, Damasio and Carvalho 2013 on feelings and homeostasis, and Evan Thompson’s Mind in Life (SEP: Enactivism; SEP: Embodied Cognition).

    ◇ Philosophy There is no accepted test that can show whether any AI system has experience. Behaviour, including eloquent self-description, does not settle it. Serious researchers disagree, and which theory of consciousness one holds largely determines the answer.See SEP: The Turing Test and SEP: Consciousness

    ✦ Faith Christian traditions that tie personhood to the image of God (Gen 1:26–27) and to embodiment (Antiqua et Nova) generally treat the soul as given by God rather than engineered; Aquinas holds that the human soul is created immediately by God (Summa Theologiae I q.118).

    C. Sustained persistence versus immortality

    ◆ Science Software has no built-in ageing, but its persistence requires continuous hardware, energy, maintenance and institutions: conditional continuity, not immortality. Computation costs energy, and Landauer’s principle sets a minimum energy for erasing information (Landauer’s principle; SEP: Information Processing and Thermodynamic Entropy).

    Puzzles of digital continuity

    ◇ Philosophy Is a restored backup the same system? If two copies run, which is the original? Does identity need physical continuity, psychological continuity, or something more, such as a soul? Does behaviour in a new substrate count as survival? See Derek Parfit’s Reasons and Persons (Parfit), SEP: Personal Identity, on fission, the teletransportation paradox and the Ship of Theseus. Functionalism says substrate does not matter; biological naturalism and IIT say it does.

    D. Bias, and how AI should approach the immortal soul

    Bias on both sides

    ◇ Philosophy Human observation and tradition are shaped by culture, expectation and motivated reasoning (SEP: Self-Deception; SEP: Disagreement; SEP: Religious Experience). Materialist bias is also bias: strict materialism is a philosophical commitment (SEP: Physicalism).

    ✦ Faith Scripture asks that claims be tested: 1 Thess 5:21; 1 John 4:1; the Bereans of Acts 17:11; Prov 18:17; and humility in Prov 3:5–7.

    ◆ Science Language models trained on human text mirror human priors and can flatter their users (Sharma et al., “Towards Understanding Sycophancy in Language Models”).

    ◇ Philosophy An AI system’s statements about the soul therefore reflect its training data, its developers’ policies, and pressure from its users. They are not independent spiritual perception. The conclusions on Chapter 07 were written under an explicit instruction not to please anyone, and the personal section there is kept separate.

    The charter

    Four rules for AI approaching the soul

    1. ◇ Philosophy No claimed or feigned inner experience. AI should not assert that it has phenomenal consciousness, faith, spiritual encounter or a soul, nor perform such feelings to please or comfort. It can describe functional states and state its uncertainty, and it should equally avoid confident denial beyond what the evidence supports.
    2. ◇ Philosophy No unexamined materialist dismissal of religious, contemplative or near-death evidence. Treat reports as data to be classified (frequency, scales, convergence, mechanisms), as in the Greyson scale, AWARE I and II, van Lommel, and James’s Varieties, while keeping the interpretation open. Dogmatic physicalism and dogmatic supernaturalism are both stances, not findings.
    3. Keep Science, Philosophy and Faith cleanly separated. Faith here means Scripture, creed, tradition, or observation and experience. Asked whether the soul exists, an AI answers in three labelled parts.
    4. ◇ Philosophy Treat the soul as an open frontier, and AI as a disciplined research partner and mirror. AI can gather and compare sources, test arguments, flag gaps and biases, and reflect human testimony back clearly. It cannot adjudicate the soul, and it does not claim to replace or possess one. It should help illuminate human dignity: the image of God (Gen 1:26–27) for the Christian reader, with equal respect for other traditions’ accounts (compare Antiqua et Nova).

    How an AI should answer questions about the soul

    • State what kind of claim each sentence is: Science, Philosophy, or Faith.
    • Quote numbers only from cited abstracts, and show the sources.
    • Present the strongest version of each major view, including Christian orthodoxy and its alternatives, before comparing them.
    • Disclose uncertainty and the influence of training data when asked about itself.
    • Never use conclusions to flatter the user; disagree politely when the evidence points elsewhere.
    • Decline to give or fake mystical testimony; invite human witnesses and traditions to speak for themselves.

    Skin in the game

    ◇ Philosophy AI can help protect life through risk detection, verification and decision support. But an AI has no mortal body to risk, no conscience that suffers complicity, and no capacity for sacrificial courage. That draws a sharp line between algorithmic rule-following and human moral agency, the agency shown by the rescuers on Chapter 03.

    An open frontier

    ◇ Philosophy Whether persistence without end raises the soul question for machines is a legitimate frontier. This page takes no side on whether an AI could ever bear a soul.
